# White Chess, Touching Grass

## 2026-09-17

Some scattered reflections:

I appreciate how Pippin’s book manages to be both breezy to read and thought-provoking right off the bat. I’ve been “out of the game” for a while (no pun intended) so an accessible onramp back towards critical design practice feels perfect right now. The example Pongs discussed in this chapter reflect a really nice spread of approaches and aesthetics (in the sense of how games _mean_) and I’m feeling pretty inspired!

One of the things that got me thinking was the mention of Yoko Ono’s White Chess/Play It By Trust. Obviously it’s a touchstone in game studies as an early example of how meaning is evinced through play, but one thing I feel is sometimes lost in these discussions is the _specific_ meaning of Play It By Trust. Yoko took chess, a game of war, and subverted it to create a pretty potent anti-war message. (Whether it actually succeeded in its time as political intervention is another question.)

I also see that reading in _Trace Pong_: something normally competitive is unsettled into something ambiguously collaborative. But if Pong is modeled after sport rather than war, does this unsettling carry the same political valence? To be clear, I don’t think Trace Pong _needs_ to work in this way, and I presume Pippin didn’t have any specific intentions in mind when he was making *PONGS* (beyond a readiness to be surprised by what emerged). Nor did he need to! Trace Pong is interesting and stands on its own, as well as in conversation with the other Pongs of PONGS.

That said, I think it’s interesting that he returned to the idea this year with his Super Mario Bros. hack titled explicitly *Play It By Trust*. That one raised even more questions for me: on one level it’s funny and a little abject, as so many of Pippin’s games are. But at the same time it evokes (at least to me) even less the relational aesthetics of Fluxus (especially as a single-player game), let alone the political practice of Yoko Ono. In fact, the first thing that comes to mind for me is the performance of blindfolded SMB speedrunners at Games Done Quick marathons. (And maybe a little more distantly, Cory Arcangel’s Super Mario Clouds hack, though beyond the medium of the SMB ROM and a vague thematic resonance that connection feels more tenuous.)

I guess all these questions are swirling around for me because I’ve been thinking lately about how games (and art more broadly) can meet the political moment, the Real, how our practice of design can avoid falling into the trap of working like we are divorced from the world we live in. We seem ever-ready to critique “art for art’s sake,” so why don’t we act like it?
