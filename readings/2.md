# Design and Recuperation

## 2026-10-01

I haven’t read this text in many years, so it was fun to reflect on how my own perspective has changed over time. I really appreciate Schön’s insights about process, and a lot of them continue to resonate with my personal practice.

At the same time (and in keeping with my last reading reflection), one serious issue I have is with the lack of substantive social critique when Schön describes his observations from ethnographic or embedded studies. Let’s take his anecdote about 3M: he describes the release to market of a commodity, the creative détournement or reappropriation as people find new and unexpected uses for it, and finally the *recuperation* of these uses by the corporation as new products, new market verticals, new avenues of commodification.

Of course, Schön doesn’t use these terms. Instead he situates this cycle as a holistic part of the design process. I think there’s a world where that could be true… But in the real world we live in, where terms like enshittification have entered the vernacular, where trillion-dollar corporations are built on the backs of those whose unpaid labour (creative and otherwise) is the foundation of closely-guarded training datasets, I think we’d be remiss to avoid talking about this stuff.

Even closer to home, we see these cycles of creative reappropriation and recuperation in games. Take Mario Maker. We start with Super Mario World romhacking, a wholly uncommodified community of practice. Hacks are simple at first, then the community develops more and more elaborate tools to empower others to make more interesting, complex levels. The “kaizo” style of hacks emerges, becoming a broader cultural phenomenon, reaching new audiences through grassroots cultural events like Games Done Quick. Finally, Nintendo swoops in (possibly issuing some cease and desist letters, as is its wont) and recuperates this creative labour in the commodified form of Mario Maker, which is a huge commercial success. Is this design?

Certainly the designers at Nintendo brought some meaningful differences to this new product. But there can be no meaningful discussion of these, since it is absolutely out of the question for Nintendo to even acknowledge the “questionable” design work of the romhackers as work. Mario is, was, and will always be Nintendo’s, and any reappropriation on the part of others was a wholly contingent process, which at any moment could become ripe to be plucked and transformed into a new commodity gratis. There are plenty of examples of this relationship in other spheres, but I think it’s especially worth scrutinizing in games since it’s so common (consider DOTA, Team Fortress, etc…).

I feel like I have a lot more to say about this text — whether  “good design” as it’s discussed here is applicable to art, the untroubled notion of “design thinking” in general, questions of novice versus experienced practitioners only given the briefest of mentions in this interview, and ideas around tool design via the Growltiger and McCavity anecdote (something near and dear to my heart these past few years). But I think I’ve already blown past the word count…
