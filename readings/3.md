# Designers Work

## 2026-11-05

Okay, so... true to form, I've already taken way more than 300 words of notes, since this was a 27-page paper covering a fair bit of ground. Let me see if I can corral some of the more lucid ones into something resembling a journal entry.

First off, over the course of this semester I've been trying to come to a better articulation of why I've rankled a bit at the generally untroubled notion of "design" presented in these readings. I think there's a kind of sui generis, exceptional quality to this presentation. And as I mentioned in my reflection on Schön, an important way this manifests is as a lack of substantive social critique. Since the "design" in these articles is typically industrial design or client work (rather than, say, design as one part of a holistic artistic process), this lack becomes a big stumbling block for me. (I don't really think Lim et al's cursory mention of _social responsibility_ -- borrowing the anodyne formulation of "corporate social responsibility" favored by some of the most vile companies on the planet -- really counts.)

What's more, I think there's a disconnect from the lived reality of designers in these texts that manifests in a few ways.

(One of the smaller but more amusing bits of idealism -- which in fairness is probably less a corollary to the broader disconnect from reality I want to critique, and more the exuberance of the era this paper comes from -- is this notion that digital materials are "without qualities" and inhabit an "infinite design space." I can appreciate that digital design comes with a very different set of constraints than non-digital design and that it might be a difficult proposition to try to find a unifying principle that bridges these worlds. But they seem to have just given up here rather than attempting to approach the question rigourously... or at least admitting that it was an open question beyond the scope of their paper.)

Anyway, the thing I really wanted to talk about was: most designers work! They live in the real world, and they are often far more constrained by factors out of their control (budget, management caprice) which shape and narrow the design space far more than the designer is able to themselves. At my last industry job I worked in a medium-size (~100-person) studio as a designer. We prototyped in earnest for weeks, going through rounds of iteration, documenting, amending. But the overdetermining, overriding factor in the design was the studio founder-cum-creative director helicoptering in, declaring sudden changes in direction. Sometimes he'd work late at night when everyone had gone home, rewriting broad swathes of design documents or story bibles overnight and leaving hapless workers to try to pick up the pieces afterwards. (His nickname among a good chunk of the company was "Hurricane Alex.")

The situation needn't always be this dramatic. A friend of mine has intermittently worked as a graphic designer on less-prestigious gigs: grocery store flyers, funeral home pamphlets. In these situations the caprice of a boss might not be quite as extreme, but instead the overriding requirement is to colour within the lines, to not stray outside of what's expected of you. So again I ask: is this design?
