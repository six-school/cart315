# Prototype 3: Walking Sim?

Thinking about approaches to this prototype. I've made a few "ambient exploration experiences" in Unity before... I'm not necessarily uninterested in making another one, but I'd also like to try something a bit different.

Lately I've been fascinated with the [M-VAVE FM-1](https://www.m-vave.com/product?id=fm-1), a tiny Chinese synth with onboard battery and speaker that can be had for around $60CAD. It emulates the Yamaha DX7 synth that was big in the 80s (and that has relevance to games: it's the grandaddy of the beloved Genesis/Mega Drive YM2612 sound chip).

The FM-1 got pretty popular earlier this year as a cheap, fun portable synth. This fall, people started reverse-engineering its firmware. A [Japanese synth hardware and software designer](https://hugelton.com/) released his [alternative firmware](https://github.com/hugelton/Felucca) less than a month ago under the GPL. When I tried it out on the FM-1 that I got over the summer it was a minor revelation: the device went from being a fun, limited synth with a nostalgic sound to a fully-featured 4-track groovebox with over a dozen full-fat non-FM synthesis engines. I've been pretty astonished at the kinds of noises this tiny, cheap device can produce now.

What's more, since that first open alternative firmware came out there's been a wild Cambrian explosion of people experimenting with the device. I've never seen anything quite like it; in just a few weeks [dozens on dozens of custom firmwares](https://github.com/aroum/fm1-custom-fw) have been released. Most of them have [web versions](https://hugelton.github.io/Felucca/webapp/try/) that emulate the hardware so you can try them out right in the browser (with full MIDI support, no less). People have released [experimental bitimbral synths](https://oscillr.github.io/DUELL/emu/) and [knockoffs of boutique devices](https://quixotic7.github.io/ChoralRootFM1/emu/) that cost twenty times more than the FM-1. I can't keep up.

The underbelly of this, of course, is that it's all turbocharged by LLMs. A few weeks back the author of the original open firmware [wrote about his mixed feelings](https://note.com/kurogedelix/n/n225a8e2374cd) on AI-assisted development and announced he was stepping back from the project. To an approximation, every project in this new community is developed with AI assistance -- usually overtly, but not always. Most people bring their own sensibilities and tastes (I especially like [this person's hand-drawn animations](https://github.com/AntonioAltea/fmel-1) they made for their personal Mellotron firmware) and the things people make generally don't feel like slop (modulo some of the documentation that is irritatingly tinged with LLM-isms). The original author's decision to license his firmware under the GPL has helped ensure that derivatives are contributed back to the commons. Overall, I think the consequences of this whirlwind of development have been mostly positive. But the situation is still strange.

--

What does all this have to do with game prototyping? As Vadim was talking about audio design in class on Thursday, I thought it would be interesting to use my FM-1 to generate some soundscapes for a first-person game.

But then I got to thinking: what if the game itself embedded _the entire synthesizer_ and used it to generate sound reactively? It would open up some really intriguing affordances. I already know FM-1 custom firmware can run in other environments, since it was ported to the browser. The device is pretty underpowered, so it _probably_ wouldn't be a strain on performance for the average computer. Godot has a well-documented C++ extension SDK. And I know that some projects that use it, like [godot-rust](https://godot-rust.github.io/), can even build for web.


Except that I'm not enough of a hand at C/C++ to pull something like that off, nevermind the expertise that would be needed with Godot's SDK and whatever toolchain the FM-1 firmware uses. But... maybe this is a good test case to actually investigate AI-assisted development at scale myself. I feel like I have a pretty well-formed critique of the tech (as capitalist imposition, as site of primary accumulation of heretofore uncommodified social relations, as mass displacer of labour, as ecological disaster, etc.) but I still have a desire to better understand the dialectic of how it acts on people and how they act back. (Some of the strange new social phenomena around access to code I've seen this year -- such as people developing low-level firmare en masse -- have also fed that curiosity.)

I've tried out LLMs for a few small one-off things (utility scripts to process files, synthesizing answers to questions that wouldn't easily be searchable through normal means). This month I dipped my toe a bit further by getting Claude to build a flashcard web app for language study for one of my other classes. But with my background in web dev, I knew what the scope of that project would be. I knew what was possible ahead of time, I specified the exact set of tools and technologies I wanted to use, and I read all the code it produced (which came out to under 700 lines).

For this kind of extension, I'd have far less idea what's going on under the hood. I'd have to defer to the AI to investigate whether this is even possible. I guess that's the first question to answer.

The other thing is that Vadim asked students to not use LLMs for their prototypes. I have no interest in having an LLM generate gameplay code or graphics or environments or whatever, but I feel like low-level library code is... maybe okay? A lot of components of Unity itself are written with AI assistance now, for example... (My friend who works at Unity Montreal told me the other week that he hasn't written a line of code by hand in months.) And if hunch is right, it could work as a compiled binary addon, so _technically_ there'd be no LLM-generated code in this prototype or this repo... just some new engine affordances.

I'm also conscientious that this investigation probably shouldn't be the primary focus of this prototyping process and of my reflections (I say, a thousand words deep into the prototype process journal).

## The Actual Prototype Idea

This summer I played this game [Moldrise](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4519130/MOLDRISE/) -- I have my critiques of it, but I really liked its look and premise. And I'd love to see more short-form work in the "knowledge game" genre like that. Anyway, I was thinking of how a tenement stairwell is a pretty interesting environment, especially for how minimal and constrained it is: all these doors into other lives, other private worlds. (Reminds me a bit of Warren Spector's old "One City Block" idea -- though just to be clear I have no such grandiose ambitions for this prototype.)

I saw Argento's Inferno recently, which is centered around a mysterious New York apartment building full of strange, secretive tenants. And I was also reflecting the other day on From Software's Echo Night games on PS1/PS2, where you're pulled into ghosts' memories of the past, sometimes through everyday objects, sometimes into surreal scenes. All of that congealed into this idea of a tenement stairwell full of doors, some of which might open up into surreal landscapes, each with their own soundscapes. Maybe a bit wishy-washy, but I think wishy-washy vibes are enough to get started on for this assignment... the surprises will emerge from there.

## 2026-10-23

I guess I'm doing this. I asked Claude to figure out the plausibility of what I had in mind. I have to admit that these things are really scarily capable now: it did some searches and then pulled down all the relevant repos and compiled the code, all within a virtual machine instance in the cloud while I interacted from the browser. There were a bunch of rounds of iteration while I narrowed in on the affordances I needed and what trade-offs I thought were acceptable, sometimes by elaborating an example scenario. It took the whole evening, but it confirmed it was possible and came up with a pretty plausible-sounding plan based on a few test scenarios it built and compiled itself.

Afterwards I wondered whether the whole exchange might have been an extended hallucination and it had done nothing except pretend to compile and debug code. But it seems like could work.

## 2026-10-24

It works. It built a Godot extension that runs the synth. I was able to take one of my FM-1 projects off the device and put it into the game, and it played and sounded exactly the same. Incredible.

As a proof of concept it's good, but there's no actual affordances beyond playing a synthesizer sequence so far. Now to draw the rest of the owl.

## 2026-11-01

The week flew by -- did some work on this project and documented what I could in commits, but evidently I wasn't diligent with journaling. I scrawled some notes to myself elsewhere throughout the process for my own purposes, so I'll try to reconstruct some relevant thoughts here... Maybe it's easiest to walk through it all by area of work.

### Level Design

I made a rough tenement stairwell environment, with a few rooms and corridors branching off the main chamber. One very successful aspect of this was that I opted to use the open-source extension [Anvil](https://anvilleveldesign.com/) to build the levels. It's a Trenchbroom/Hammer-style design tool, and it immediately felt intuitive and comfortable to me.

I never did more than dabble briefly in "traditional" FPS level design, but I gave Trenchbroom another shake earlier this year since both [Bevy](https://github.com/Noxmore/bevy_trenchbroom) and [Godot](https://github.com/func-godot/func_godot_plugin) support it as a map format. (Also because I love the Quake lighting model!) As a level design tool for a game from the 90s I think it's great, but viewed through a more contemporary lens I think it forces you to hew too closely to the specific idiosyncrasies of the Quake engine far too much. The constraints feel arbitrary and harsh to me instead of generative.

Anvil, on the other hand, feels like a perfect fit for for my process. I love that it's well-integrated with all the normal Blender stuff, so you have access to the whole gamut of modeling tools as soon as you need to add details to your level geometry -- or you can build and add whole models on the spot. Compared to the relative arcaneness of, say, making a simple archway in Trenchbroom (which has led to some odd strains of conspicuous showmanship in Quake level design that make no sense outside the community), Blender makes this both easy and expressive.

Anvil feels like an especially good fit for Godot: like Unity, the latter has some CSG prototyping tools, but they're clunky to use and lack texture support, amongst other shortcomings. By contrast, Anvil seems to work great for start-to-finish level design in addition to prototyping. Some of the touches (like a "quick re-export" button) are incredibly thoughtful and help make iteration super-fast, too. I'm really glad I gave it a proper shot.

The level is quite small, but I'm not unsatisfied with what I was able to lay down with relatively little effort. If I had more time, I'd add doors (including ones that don't open), add a few more intervening rooms so points of interest aren't directly adjacent to each other, break a few things off the grid a bit and make the "cave" area more organic, add more incidental details for flavour... I'm not sure any of that is critical for this prototype, except insofar as it would lend it more atmosphere (which is admittedly important for a walking sim!). Speaking of atmosphere...

### Sound

I threw some FM1 sequences in the environment as a first test, and that's what was still in there when I demoed in class on Thursday. I don't think they're very good and I want to change them! They don't really build a mood. They're just... very short, somewhat annoying loops. I muted the drum track in the main room, which I think helps a bit.

It feels bad that I spent so much of my initial time on this prototype working on a unique audio system and then didn't get time to really dig into what interesting stuff it might actually afford designers. Maybe it was a bad fit for the walking sim prototype brief. I don't regret doing it, but I second-guessed myself on this more than once.

Reflecting on interactive audio this past week, I think there's a few levels to how it can be approached:

1. Taking prerecorded audio and passing it through an FFT. Best suited to visualizing audio, though I guess if you carefully set up your FFT buckets you could theoretically use it as a source for interactive elements in the game (I assume Vib Ribbon and other "bring your own music" games effectively do this). I think splitting a track into stems and fading them in and out situationally is conceptually on this level, too. You can go far with this approach!

2. Prerecorded audio with accompanying markup file. I saw recently that there's a [Godot add-on for MIDI files](https://github.com/nlaha/godot-midi) for exactly this end. I assume most Bemani-ish music games work something like this, but it also makes some sense to me for a game like Rez, or potentially even something like that Hi-Fi Rush game (which also makes extensive use of stems and other techniques).

3. Generative audio. It could be sample-based rather than synthesis, but the important part is that the game has some fine level of control over what sounds play when, and _how_ they sound. I think it's probably in continuity with the other levels rather than a clean and bright line (after all, isn't any game that plays sound effects in response to player actions generative...?). Lots of possibilities here: there's stuff like re-keying notes, time-scaling them... For synthesized audio there's also tweaking generator parameters proper. There's also the possibility of real-time effects, though many game engines already support this to some extent.

The audio add-on I came up with is _in theory_ in the third category, but as of right now it's not doing anything that wouldn't be at least as easy in the first category.

I did have a few things I had wanted to try:

- Player footstep sounds that manifest as drum hits in time with the music.

- Apartment doors you knock on (a drum fill) and that open on the next bar, sweeping up lowpass filter cutoff. I actually prototyped this in 2D while developing the library, but didn't get to rebuilding and integrating it for the prototype proper.

- Snapping a melody to a scale and transposing up and down by (snapped) semitones as the player goes up and down the stairs in the main room. (The FM-1 core already supports both scale snapping and transposition, so this is "built-in" and would be quick to try.)

- Collecting parts of a song (fallen stars?) in the skybox room, letting the player build up a musical track by pieces. Kaisa's prototype from class inspired me here too! Rather than just gradually "unmuting" pieces of audio, I think it could be cool to try, say, changing a melody and increasing its intensity by using Euclidean sequencing and gradually adding notes. (Euclidean sequencing is also built into the firmware!)

- "Spicing" (to borrow a term from Arturia gear) a melody randomly as the player explores a sub-area. I'm curious if this would create interesting, emergent affect if it occurs as the result of player actions.. If nothing else at least it might produce a sense of movement and variety from a relatively short initial piece of music. (Sequence mutation is also built into the firmware!!)

I'm really excited to give all of these a shot at some point -- I think there's a good spread of low-hanging fruit here. Just need to find a few more hours...

### Graphics

I was a little despondent at demo time on Thursday because I felt like my demo looked _terrible_. I've seen great-looking Godot games in the wild, but the out-of-the-box rendering seemed inexplicably awful. What's more, my first attempts at baked lighting somehow made things _even worse_. It took a while to realize that shadows are just... disabled by default? For both dynamic and static lights. (I think they might even be disabled only for _some kinds of lights_, which is even more confusing -- not going to dig around to confirm right now.) There's some more quirks I had to puzzle out... I already left a wordy commit message about this, so I won't repeat myself more.

The upshot for me, in keeping with my realization from previous prototype journal, was that spending a bit of time on look can be both motivating and sometimes unreasonably impactful, especially when mood is the goal. I was just about ready to throw my level in the trash and do something that didn't lean on lighting or shading so much. (I came up with a different and more Bernband-inspired idea, which does call out to me somewhat...)

The textures I chose (all grabbed from Polyhaven, but for expediency I only used the albedo/diffuse channel of each) were selected somewhat haphazardly, so there's definitely more work that could be done. But the place this ended up visually feels like a good stopping point for me for now.

### Miscellaneous

Like the Usagi prototypes, I was able to get the Godot prototype to build for web on every push and deploy to GitHub Pages. I gotta say, this rules. It's great to be able to see something you want to change, make a quick tweak, commit and push it, and watch it go live without having to do a rebuild locally or upload anything else. With this "monorepo" setup, all the prototypes are available on the same site, so it's a nice scrapbook of all my classwork in one place.

It's especially cool that native extensions for Godot can work with web builds too... I feel like web exports became more and more marginal for Unity over time, so seeing it work so robustly in Godot is heartening.

I did file a bug in the Godot repo re: an obscure issue with web export (which I was able to work around). I think that makes the tally so far one issue on Godot, three issues (and a long comment on an existing issue) on Usagi, and two on Colyseus. Not complaining -- better than the black hole of commercial software bug reports.

I didn't go into detail here, but the brief AI-assisted leg of development was by far the most strange (at times bordering on frightening) part of all this. I think overall I'm glad to have gone deep on it, but I'm going to have to spend some time with myself unpacking that side further.
