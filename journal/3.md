# Prototype 3: Walking Sim?

Thinking about approaches to this prototype. I've made a few "ambient exploration experiences" in Unity before... I'm not necessarily uninterested in making another one, but I'd also like to try something a bit different.

Lately I've been fascinated with the [M-VAVE FM-1](https://www.m-vave.com/product?id=fm-1), a tiny Chinese synth with onboard battery and speaker that can be had for around $60CAD. It emulates the Yamaha DX7 synth that was big in the 80s (and that has relevance to games: it's the grandaddy of the beloved Genesis/Mega Drive YM2612 sound chip).

The FM-1 got pretty popular earlier this year as a cheap, fun portable synth. This fall, people started reverse-engineering its firmware. A [Japanese synth hardware and software designer](https://hugelton.com/) released his [alternative firmware](https://github.com/hugelton/Felucca) less than a month ago under the GPL. When I tried it out on the FM-1 that I got over the summer it was a minor revelation: the device went from being a fun, limited synth with a nostalgic sound to a fully-featured 4-track groovebox with over a dozen full-fat non-FM synthesis engines. I've been pretty astonished at the kinds of noises this tiny, cheap device can produce now.

What's more, since that first open alternative firmware came out there's been a wild Cambrian explosion of people experimenting with the device. I've never seen anything quite like it; in just a few weeks [dozens on dozens of custom firmwares](https://github.com/aroum/fm1-custom-fw) have been released. Most of them have [web versions](https://hugelton.github.io/Felucca/webapp/try/) that emulate the hardware so you can try them out right in the browser (with full MIDI support, no less). People have released [experimental bitimbral synths](https://oscillr.github.io/DUELL/emu/) and [knockoffs of boutique devices](https://quixotic7.github.io/ChoralRootFM1/emu/) that cost twenty times more than the FM-1. I can't keep up.

The underbelly of this, of course, is that it's all turbocharged by LLMs. A few weeks back the author of the original open firmware [wrote about his mixed feelings](https://note.com/kurogedelix/n/n225a8e2374cd) on AI-assisted development and announced he was stepping back from the project. To an approximation, every project in this new community is developed with AI assistance -- usually overtly, but not always. Most people bring their own sensibilities and tastes (I especially like [this person's hand-drawn animations](https://github.com/AntonioAltea/fmel-1) they made for their personal Mellotron firmware) and the things people make generally don't feel like slop (modulo some of the documentation that is irritatingly tinged with LLM-isms). The original author's decision to license his firmware under the GPL has helped ensure that derivatives are contributed back to the commons. Overall, I think the consequences of this whirlwind of development have been mostly positive. But the situation is still strange.

--

What does all this have to do with game prototyping? As Vadim was talking about audio design in class on Thursday, I thought it would be interesting to use my FM-1 to generate some soundscapes for a first-person game.

But then I got to thinking: what if the game itself embedded _the entire synthesizer_ and used it to generate sound reactively? It would open up some really intriguing affordances. I already know FM-1 custom firmware can run in other environments, since it was ported to the browser. The device is pretty underpowered, so it _probably_ wouldn't be a strain on performance for the average computer. Godot has a well-documented C++ extension SDK. And I know that some projects that use it, like [godot-rust](https://godot-rust.github.io/), can even build for web.


Except that I'm not enough of a hand at C/C++ to pull something like that off, nevermind the expertise that would be needed with Godot's SDK and whatever toolchain the FM-1 firmware uses. But... maybe this is a good test case to actually investigate AI-assisted development at scale myself. I feel like I have a pretty well-formed critique of the tech (as capitalist imposition, as site of primary accumulation of heretofore uncommodified social relations, as mass displacer of labour, as ecological disaster, etc.) but I still have a desire to better understand the dialectic of how it acts on people and how they act back. (Some of the strange new social phenomena around access to code I've seen this year -- such as people developing low-level firmare en masse -- have also fed that curiosity.)

I've tried out LLMs for a few small one-off things (utility scripts to process files, synthesizing answers to questions that wouldn't easily be searchable through normal means). This month I dipped my toe a bit further by getting Claude to build a flashcard web app for language study for one of my other classes. But with my background in web dev, I knew what the scope of that project would be. I knew what was possible ahead of time, I specified the exact set of tools and technologies I wanted to use, and I read all the code it produced (which came out to under 700 lines).

For this kind of extension, I'd have far less idea what's going on under the hood. I'd have to defer to the AI to investigate whether this is even possible. I guess that's the first question to answer.

The other thing is that Vadim asked students to not use LLMs for their prototypes. I have no interest in having an LLM generate gameplay code or graphics or environments or whatever, but I feel like low-level library code is... maybe okay? A lot of components of Unity itself are written with AI assistance now, for example... (My friend who works at Unity Montreal told me the other week that he hasn't written a line of code by hand in months.) And if hunch is right, it could work as a compiled binary addon, so _technically_ there'd be no LLM-generated code in this prototype or this repo... just some new engine affordances.

I'm also conscientious that this investigation probably shouldn't be the primary focus of this prototyping process and of my reflections (I say, a thousand words deep into the prototype process journal).

Oh yeah, about the actual prototype idea.

## The Actual Prototype Idea

This summer I played this game [Moldrise](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4519130/MOLDRISE/) -- I have my critiques of it, but I really liked its look and premise. And I'd love to see more short-form work in the "knowledge game" genre like that. Anyway, I was thinking of how a tenement stairwell is a pretty interesting environment, especially for how minimal and constrained it is: all these doors into other lives, other private worlds. (Reminds me a bit of Warren Spector's old "One City Block" idea -- though just to be clear I have no such grandiose ambitions for this prototype.)

I saw Argento's Inferno recently, which is centered around a mysterious New York apartment building full of strange, secretive tenants. And I was also reflecting the other day on From Software's Echo Night games on PS1/PS2, where you're pulled into ghosts' memories of the past, sometimes through everyday objects, sometimes into surreal scenes. All of that congealed into this idea of a tenement stairwell full of doors, some of which might open up into surreal landscapes, each with their own soundscapes. Maybe a bit wishy-washy, but I think wishy-washy vibes are enough to get started on for this assignment... the surprises will emerge from there.

## 2026-10-23

I guess I'm doing this. I asked Claude to figure out the plausibility of what I had in mind. I have to admit that these things are really scarily capable now: it did some searches and then pulled down all the relevant repos and compiled the code, all within a virtual machine instance in the cloud while I interacted from the browser. There were a bunch of rounds of iteration while I narrowed in on the affordances I needed and what trade-offs I thought were acceptable, sometimes by elaborating an example scenario. It took the whole evening, but it confirmed it was possible and came up with a pretty plausible-sounding plan based on a few test scenarios it built and compiled itself.

Afterwards I wondered whether the whole exchange might have been an extended hallucination and it had done nothing except pretend to compile and debug code. But it seems like could work.

## 2026-10-24

It works. It built a Godot extension that runs the synth. I was able to take one of my FM-1 projects off the device and put it into the game, and it played and sounded exactly the same. Incredible.

As a proof of concept it's good, but there's no actual affordances beyond playing a synthesizer sequence so far. Now to draw the rest of the owl.

## 2026-10-26

It's done. Scheduling musical notes synchronized with in-game events, controlling the tempo, sweeping parameters like LPF cutoff... it all works. Even the web build works. All it took was a few days of exhausting, uncomfortable symbiosis with the machine. I had a nightmare about it last night. I think I'll unpack that more in a different journal.

The documentation it wrote is very bad and needs to be completely rewritten from scratch. I've barely dared to look at the code it wrote, but the code _I_ wrote to test it worked frighteningly well. The rest can wait until after the prototype.
