### Tama Life

Tamagotchi-style virtual pet, except the Tamagotchi has to work a job (off-screen) and pay rent.

That sounds like The Sims… except the latter is an idealized fantasy where “hard work” is always rewarded, a kind of meritocratic dollhouse. Instead I’d want to focus on the abjectness of the situation: Tamagotchi checking job websites daily, Tamagotchi’s draining bank account, Tamagotchi’s landlord not fixing the broken appliances in Tamagotchi’s apartment, not turning on the heat, painting over the mold in Tamagotchi’s bathroom…

Imagining appropriating the aesthetics of the virtual pet, low-res, abstracted/cute graphics, juxtaposed with the harsher realities of working adult life. For a long time I’ve been fascinated by the life sim [Ganbare Neo Poke-Kun](https://www.mobygames.com/game/29519/ganbare-neo-poke-kun/) and have wanted to make something in that vein. [Little Computer People](https://www.mobygames.com/game/9241/little-computer-people/) is also a big inspiration, but there’s something appealing about the single-room virtual pet approach. Beyond the simplicity it affords, I keep thinking there might be some kind of anti-immersive Brechtian distancing effect to the virtual pet framing mechanism. We see oppressive systems impose themselves upon this character who is not-us — is the player moved to anger? A sense of solidarity?

Mechanically, the virtual pet’s state could be reflected though a set of status bars — mental health (stress, anxiety), physical health (body temperature, fatigue), bank balance, fridge contents, etc. etc.

Neo Poke-Kun’s random events are an inspiration here as well (though in that game the character’s state is modeled in a very simple fashion, and the events are player-triggered and deliberately chaotic).

Ultimately this is an idea I’d like to prototype, but the representational aspect is something I’m having a hard time visualizing. Neo Poke-Kun is “8-bit” but has elaborate animation and pretty detailed pixel art… on the other hand, the original Tamagotchi graphics are _so_ abstracted that some concrete situations feel like they’d be difficult to depict. (I do have a soft spot for [Wreckboy](https://videochess.itch.io/wreckboy), which might be an inspiration somewhere between those two poles.) Regardless, it feels to me that for the (play) aesthetics to succeed, the (visual) aesthetics would need to be present in some form; I don’t think a list of status bars and a text log of events without graphics would have nearly as much impact.

Beyond the problem of represenation, I’m not sure a “zero-player” game is the right kind of thing for an early prototyping project in this class if we’re supposed to demonstrate a holistic understanding of game design. But maybe that’s overthinking it… And maybe sticking to simple, constrained graphics would be an interesting way to prototype this quickly after all… unsure.

### Degentrification Gnome

Evicted from your apartment and turned into a small gnome by a horrible twist of fate, you vow to exact revenge on the landlord’s son who now resides in your former home (at least until the landlord finds some sap to take the place at triple the rent you were paying). Set up Rube Goldberg-esque chains of traps to oust the evicters!

I’m fascinated by [Tecmo’s Deception series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tecmo's_Deception%3A_Invitation_to_Darkness), which are painfully close to being great (but never quite seem to congeal into the experience I envision when I consider the premise). I feel like those games have a lot of misdirected effort and generally fail to understand the appeal of their own mechanics, which by the second game seemingly crystallized at a local maximum. (That said, the first is fascinatingly janky and has all these builder sim elements that were later removed — well worth the price of admission on ambition and strangeness alone.) I’ve never actually finished any of them, but having peeked at some longplays it seems like there are usually one or two dominant strategies that are static over the course of the game.

Instead, I’d rather focus on surprise and emergence and the comedic aspect of setting up elaborate pranks. (There’s some resonance with the 8-bit [Spy vs. Spy games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spy_vs._Spy_(1984_video_game)) too, but again I think those are interesting for entirely different reasons, eg. how play focuses around the normally verboten action of screen peeking.)

If I were to work on this I think I’d want to limit the prototype to prove out a very small, loose fragment of the idea (for example: rather than developing complex NPC AI routines, just have the NPCs behave deterministically even if it results in a less dynamic situation overall, in order to focus on the player-driven emergent aspect). It still feels pretty ambitious, though…

### Wizard Party

Multiplayer party game inspired by Jackbox games, Twitch Plays, and old-school JRPGs (continuing my musings from my prototype 1 reflection). Like Jackbox, there’s a shared screen plus each player uses their own device (phone or computer) as a controller. Each player takes the role of a wizard in an all-wizard RPG party, fighting through a series of turn-based battles (with timed turns!). Players may have hidden information (for example, whether an enemy will attack them next turn), so they have to coordinate and plan their character’s actions in order to work together effectively.

Same as last idea, I’d like to tap into some kind of emergent comedy where things can go wrong in funny and unexpected ways — maybe a player has to bounce a fire spell off another player for maximal effect, a sort of magical volleyball spike, but if the second player misunderstood and didn’t cast the reflection magic…

I’m pretty excited by this idea and feel inclined to work on it, but I realize multi-device multiplayer could be too ambitious. Given that this isn’t a hard realtime game (hundreds of milliseconds of latency is completely fine!) I think I can probably prototype this with web tech (which has been my day job for the past decade, so not a huge reach) for an initial version. I’d probably try to avoid implementing as much “around-the-game” functionality as possible (lobbies, access codes, multiple concurrent games, anything beyond the most basic required game flow). But it might still be too much…

More than that, it feels like “finding the fun” would require a bunch of iteration, and there’s a lot of different knobs to tweak with this concept. (How many spells/affordances should the players have? How much room for error is there? How much stuff needs to be present to make creative emergence — and comedic situations! — possible? How long should turns last?) Some of these questions aren’t easily answered playtesting solo and following my own taste. But the design space is pretty exciting, so maybe it’s worth a shot regardless.

## 2026-10-08

Multiplayer game. Thinking about implementation. Small screens should be web-based so there’s nothing to download. For something interface-heavy and friendly to mobile interfaces plain old React might be a good idea… For the big screen's more game-like affordances should it be `react-three-fiber`? I’ve had reasonable experiences with it in the past, and it would make declaratively drawing things to arbitrary positions on the screen easy (as well as animating sprites, etc…)

Unsure, but for a prototype I should probably start with vanilla React (or no framework) unless it becomes overwhelmingly clear that it’ll be an obstacle. For both big screen and small screens.

I guess it needs to run on a server on the internet to avoid NAT/firewall problems, as annoying as that is… Hopefully I can spin up the server for the class demonstration and then spin it down again right after, to avoid it getting hammered by random AI crawlers.  There should probably also be a Jackbox-style join code (even if the server only runs a single session at a time) to prevent unwanted session joins.

It definitely sounds like I should get basic session management flow up and running as quickly as possible (even if there’s no actual game mechanics present) to prove out that this is technically possible and reassure myself I’m not missing any major technical blind spots here. If I can’t get that going within a day or so, I shouldn’t try to prototype the game proper.

- Server handling overall game flow, screens as thin clients
- Main game window (“big screen”) with audio, instructions, etc.
- Player screens (“small screen”) with menu choices, hidden info, etc.
- Timed rounds
- Everything over Websockets, presumably.
- Deadline Tues/Weds night?
- After technical test, figure out screens and full flow. Plunder some FF sprites and music.

Ultimately if I were to release this as a full thing I’d probably want some kind of WebRTC-based P2P setup so that no full-fledged backend server is required (so the “big screen” frontend stands up the session and acts as the authority, and others connect via a static site that takes a URL code). I think there’s libraries that can do this (leveraging free public STUN/TURN servers), but I haven’t really audited any of them or looked over the landscape in a while… regular server/client seems like the safer bet for now.

## 2026-10-12

Okay, despite my previous warning to myself, I did a small spike to test a frontend-less multiplayer setup using [PeerJS](https://github.com/peers/peerjs)… It would just be too convenient to have an un-DDOS-able setup that I could plunk on free static hosting, like Github Pages. What’s more, it would remove one degree of complexity from the networking setup (ie. `big screen/host <-> small screens` instead of `server <-> big screen` plus `server <-> small screens`).

And… PeerJS seems to work well! Except that if I disconnect my phone from wifi and try to join the session over cellular data, it can’t get in. I think that rules it out for a classroom playtest… if PeerJS doesn’t “just work” on this basic network topology out of the gate I feel like Concordia’s internet setup is a big roll of the dice.

So server-client it is… Guess I’ll use [socket.io](https://socket.io/) which is a little old and crusty at this point but from a quick look around appears to still be the best option for robustness to network hitches, automatic reconnection, etc…
